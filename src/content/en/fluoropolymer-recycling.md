## Two families, two routes

**PTFE does not melt-process.** Above its 327 °C melting point it stays far too viscous to extrude or mold like other thermoplastics, so PTFE parts are made by compression molding, ram extrusion and sintering. PTFE scrap — skived film, machining chips, offcuts and powder — is recovered by **micronization**: molecular-weight reduction followed by jet milling to fine powder, used as a lubricating additive in compounds, coatings and inks. The group micronizes PTFE in the US, Croatia and India.

**The other fluoropolymers melt.** PFA, FEP, ETFE, ECTFE, PVDF and PCTFE are reprocessed by the **melt route**, one polymer at a time:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PTFE | PTFE datasheet | 327 | 2.13–2.22 |
| PFA | Teflon™ PFA 440 HP | 302–310 | 2.12–2.17 |

## Segregation is everything

Fluoropolymers look and feel alike, and a few percent of the wrong one can ruin a melt-reprocessed lot. Keep each polymer, and each grade, in its own labeled container; keep PTFE away from the melt-processable grades; and tell us about any fillers (glass, carbon, bronze, graphite) — filled PTFE is common in seals and bearings and is handled separately from virgin-grade scrap.

## A changing supply market

Fluoropolymer supply is shifting. 3M ended all PFAS manufacturing in 2025, including its Dyneon fluoropolymers, and in 2026 agreed to sell Dyneon GmbH to an affiliate of Capmont ([European Rubber Journal](https://www.european-rubber-journal.com/article/2099282/3m-agrees-sale-of-former-dyneon-business-in-germany)). Recovered fluoropolymer — micronized PTFE and reprocessed melt-processable grades — is one way processors can secure material.
