## Question 1: does it melt?

If the polymer can be melted and reshaped, the **melt route** is almost always the best use of it: the material keeps its identity and goes back into molding or extrusion as granule or compound.

Melt-processable high-performance polymers include PEEK and the other PAEKs, PEI, thermoplastic polyimide (TPI), PSU, PESU, PPSU, PPS, LCP, PPA and the melt-processable fluoropolymers (PFA, FEP, ETFE, ECTFE, PVDF, PCTFE). PAI also belongs here — but only before post-cure.

The melt route in brief: size reduction to regrind, cleaning where needed, drying, intake testing (DSC, TGA, melt flow), twin-screw extrusion, strand cutting and release testing.

## Question 2: if it doesn't melt, how does it break?

Some high-performance materials cannot be melt-reprocessed:

- **Thermoset polyimide** (Vespel®, Kapton® film, P84®) — no melt.
- **Fully post-cured PAI** — no longer melt-processable.
- **PBI** — compression-molded, not melt-processed.
- **PTFE** — melts at 327 °C but is far too viscous to process like a thermoplastic.
- **Elastomers** such as FKM and FFKM — crosslinked.

These are recovered as **powder**, used as functional fillers that add wear resistance, lubricity or temperature resistance to compounds. The milling method is chosen by how the material behaves at room temperature, not by its chemical family:

| Behavior at room temperature | Examples | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glassy and brittle | Thermoset polyimide, post-cured PAI, PBI, phenolic | Ambient mechanical milling (hammer or attrition mill) |
| Low cohesive strength, very high molecular weight | PTFE | Molecular-weight reduction, then jet milling to micropowder |
| Rubbery | FKM, FFKM, NBR, EPDM, silicone | Cryogenic grinding |
| Very tough, highly oriented | Drawn UHMWPE and para-aramid fiber | Cryogenic grinding — or pulping or cutting instead |

### Why cryogenic grinding is the exception

Cryogenic grinding cools material with liquid nitrogen until it becomes brittle enough to fracture cleanly. It is essential for elastomers, which smear and gum an ambient mill, and useful for extremely tough drawn fibers. For materials that are already brittle at room temperature — thermoset polyimide, cured PAI, PBI — it adds liquid nitrogen cost and nothing else. Choosing ambient milling where it works is one of the simplest ways to lower recovery cost.

## Question 3: is it a fiber?

Fibers keep most of their value as fibers. Carbon fiber, para-aramid, PBO, PBI fiber, oxidized PAN and phenolic fibers are cleaned and opened, then **precision-cut, random-chopped, pulped, spun into yarn or made into nonwovens**. For carbon fiber, loose roving and off-grade bobbins are oversized before precision chopping so that bundles stay intact — see [carbon fiber sizing, oversizing and chopping](/library/carbon-fiber-sizing-oversizing-chopping/).

## The decision in one line

Melt it if it melts; mill it the way it breaks if it doesn't; keep it a fiber if it's a fiber — and segregate before you do any of them.
